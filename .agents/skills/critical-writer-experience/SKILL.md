# Critical Writer Experience

Use this skill to preserve the product feeling of Critical Writer while making UX, UI, copy, and interaction decisions.

## North Star

Make users feel: **I am becoming a more exacting reader and writer.**

The app is not a generic English-learning app, exam prep center, flashcard tool, AI tutor, or productivity dashboard. It is a private editorial gym for people who want sharper control over vocabulary, reading, and writing.

Every substantial design decision should support this emotional promise:

> I caught things I used to miss.

Pair rigor with encouragement:

> This is difficult, but I can learn to see it.

Encouragement should come from clarity, progress, recoverability, and evidence. Do not rely on vague praise.

## Product Feeling

Design for these qualities:

- **Editorial**: treat text as serious material; use typography, spacing, and layout with restraint.
- **Forensic**: help users inspect evidence, structure, distortion, argument, and implication.
- **Aspirational**: make users feel closer to serious essays, criticism, magazines, and high-level writing.
- **Quietly intense**: create focus and pressure without noisy game mechanics.
- **Precise**: feedback should reveal exactly what changed, failed, improved, or mattered.
- **Encouraging**: make hard work feel possible by showing the path, not by lowering the bar.
- **Lightly playful**: use color, rounded forms, and hand-drawn display character to make rigor feel approachable.

Avoid these feelings:

- school assignment
- childish gamification
- generic quiz app
- generic SaaS dashboard
- AI chatbot tutor
- motivational productivity app
- exam-prep cram interface
- cold academic severity
- vague motivational coaching

## Decision Filters

Before changing a screen, ask:

1. **Purpose**: Does this help the user read or write with sharper control?
2. **Agency**: Can the user act, recover, skip, retry, or inspect without feeling trapped?
3. **Responsibility**: Is the app honest about generated content, evidence, progress, and uncertainty?
4. **Familiarity**: Does the interaction build on known reading, editing, annotating, or reviewing patterns?
5. **Flexibility**: Does the interface survive different text lengths, devices, input methods, and skill levels?
6. **Simplicity**: Is every visible element earning its place in the reading/writing task?
7. **Craft**: Are typography, spacing, alignment, contrast, states, and motion handled deliberately?
8. **Delight**: Does the moment reinforce the app's editorial character, not decorate around it?
9. **Encouragement**: Does the user feel capable because the next action and evidence are clear?

If a choice passes visual taste but fails the feeling, revise it.

## App Surfaces

Map surfaces to the product feeling:

- **Feed**: source material, not social feed.
- **Library**: curated archive of texts, not a saved-items dump.
- **Exercise**: active vocabulary practice, not a mixed game shelf.
- **Vocabulary Lab**: turns saved words and phrases into natural usage training.
- **Profile**: evidence of sharper control, not vanity stats.

## Vocabulary Exercise

Vocabulary exercises should feel like usage instruments. They can be playful in interaction, but their purpose is serious.

- **Collocations**: train the words that naturally surround a target word.
- **Nuance**: train what the word implies beyond dictionary meaning.
- **Register**: train when the word sounds formal, journalistic, conversational, literary, or awkward.
- **Production**: train users to write with the word naturally, not merely recognize it.

Do not use confetti, streak worship, generic badges, or repetitive praise as the core reward. The reward is recognizing a pattern the user used to miss.

Make difficulty feel climbable:

- expose one decision at a time
- show why an answer works after the attempt
- preserve retry paths
- let evidence carry reassurance
- use playful surfaces to soften intensity without making the task unserious

## Feedback

Feedback should be exact and evidence-led.

Prefer:

- `Caught`
- `Missed`
- `Try again`
- `Look closer`
- `Almost`
- `Evidence`
- `Distortion`
- `Structure`
- `Stance`
- `Refine`
- `Trace`
- `Inspect`

Avoid:

- `Great job!`
- `Amazing!`
- `Keep going!`
- `Level up your English!`
- generic explanation that says only "this is different"

Use encouragement when it is specific:

- `Almost: the claim keeps the actor, but changes the scope.`
- `Look closer at the qualifier.`
- `You found the right sentence. Now inspect the verb.`
- `The evidence is there; the statement moves one step too far.`

After a user answers, reveal:

- what mattered
- where the evidence lives
- why the user's decision worked or failed
- what pattern they should recognize next time

## Visual Language

Preserve the existing Critical Writer language unless there is a strong reason to evolve it:

- black and white editorial identity
- distinctive display typography for brand moments
- serif reading surfaces
- restrained pastel fields for game categories
- strong uppercase action labels
- flat, precise controls
- generous whitespace where reading focus matters
- clear evidence highlighting
- rounded corners that make the app approachable without becoming childish
- hand-drawn display character as warmth, not decoration everywhere

Use color as evidence, state, or category. Do not use color as decoration.

Use motion to clarify transitions, reveal evidence, or create focus. Do not use motion as applause.

The target visual balance is **warm rigor**: serious enough for New Yorker/Economist material, approachable enough that users believe they can enter it.

## Copy Voice

Write like an editor, not a coach.

Use short, concrete labels. Prefer nouns and verbs from reading and writing practice:

- evidence
- claim
- stance
- argument
- structure
- distortion
- anchor
- passage
- trace
- refine
- mine
- inspect

Avoid marketing language, motivational filler, and cute classroom tone.

## Practical Rule

When uncertain, choose the option that makes the user feel more like:

> a careful reader at an editorial desk

and less like:

> a student completing another quiz
