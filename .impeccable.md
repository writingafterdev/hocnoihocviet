## Design Context

### Users
Reader & Writer is for serious non-native English learners, especially students and young professionals preparing for IELTS, GRE, or similar high-stakes exams. They use the app in focused study sessions to build reading comprehension, vocabulary range, and confidence from real premium journalism.

For games specifically, users are practicing with excerpt-bank content mined from authentic articles. The game UI should help them concentrate on the text, answer precisely, and feel steady progress without arcade pressure.

### Brand Personality
Clean, confident, focused. The interface should feel like a precision reading tool with a quiet editorial sensibility: serious, calm, and deliberate.

The games may be a little more kinetic than the article reader, but they must not become childish, loud, or reward-loop driven. Avoid Duolingo-style mascots, confetti, noisy celebrations, streak pressure, and cartoonish visual language.

### Aesthetic Direction
The product baseline is an editorial reading workspace: white and near-white surfaces, black ink, restrained borders, serif reading text, and small uppercase sans labels.

The games add a distinct study-lab layer:

- Use full-screen, unframed game surfaces rather than dashboard cards.
- Each game owns a soft pastel background:
  - Skim & Scan: `#D6EAF8` card color, `#EBF5FC` session background.
  - Processing: `#E8DCFC` card color, `#F4EDFE` session background.
- Keep primary controls black: `#111` background, white text, hover `#111` at about 80 percent opacity or `neutral-800`.
- Use semantic feedback only for correctness:
  - Correct: `#DDF4DF` fill with `#2E8B57` border.
  - Incorrect: `#FDE2E2` fill with `#E74C3C` border.
  - Inactive/secondary: white, transparent, or black at 5-40 percent opacity.
- Avoid gradients, glossy effects, saturated gamified palettes, and decorative color outside the game identity/background.

Typography:

- Game shell labels use sans-serif, uppercase, bold, and wide tracking: typically `text-[10px]` to `text-[13px]`, `font-sans`, `font-bold`, `uppercase`, `tracking-widest` or `tracking-[0.18em]`.
- Game titles and compact headings use `font-display` around `17px`, `20-28px`, or large hero scales on the exercise landing.
- Reading passages, answer options, prompts, and explanatory copy use `font-serif` with relaxed line-height. Passage text usually sits around `18-22px`; answer options around `17px`; helper text around `14-15px`.
- Text color is mostly `#111`; supporting text uses opacity such as `text-[#111]/40`, `/50`, `/60`, or `/70`.

Shape and spacing:

- The game dialect uses tighter radii than the broader app cards. Default interactive radius is `rounded-[10px]`; slots/options often use `rounded-[10px]` or `rounded-[12px]`; draggable hero cards can use `rounded-[16px]`.
- Buttons: primary CTA `px-8` to `px-10`, `py-3` to `py-4`, black fill, white text, uppercase sans label, radius `10px`.
- Multiple-choice options: full-width, left-aligned, `px-6 py-4`, `border-2`, radius `10px`, serif `17px`, clear selected state as black fill with white text.
- Keep layouts centered and constrained: main game content usually `max-w-[800px]`; passages and option stacks often `max-w-[600px]` to `max-w-[700px]`.
- Use generous vertical rhythm: `gap-8`, `gap-10`, `gap-12`, with thin dividers `border-[#111]/10` or `/20`.

Motion and interaction:

- Motion should be purposeful and state-based: fade/slide between phases, short crossfades, progress bar updates, card drag feedback, countdown scaling.
- Use `motion/react` transitions in the existing language: `opacity`, `x/y`, `scale`, and clip-path circle wipe for entering games.
- Avoid continuous decorative motion except for game-specific instructional/interactive illustrations.
- Keep interaction states legible before animation polish: selected, disabled, correct, incorrect, loading, empty, and complete states should be obvious without relying only on color.

### Design Principles
1. Text first, game second. The excerpt is the product surface; controls and decoration must stay subordinate to reading.
2. Calm challenge over arcade energy. Games can feel interactive and satisfying, but never noisy, childish, or manipulative.
3. One game, one soft identity color. Use pastel backgrounds to identify modes, then rely on black, white, and semantic feedback for interaction.
4. Precision states. Every game control needs clear selected, disabled, correct, incorrect, loading, empty, and complete states.
5. Editorial typography inside playful mechanics. Serif passages and answers preserve the journalism feel; uppercase sans labels provide structure and speed.
6. Stable, centered formats. Fixed dimensions, constrained widths, and consistent radii keep the training task from shifting or feeling improvised.
